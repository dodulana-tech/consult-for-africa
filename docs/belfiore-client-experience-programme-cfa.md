## 1. What this programme is actually for

Three commercial facts sit behind the design. They are the reason the syllabus looks the way it does.

**Your enquiry channels are your sales floor.** In aesthetics, first contact arrives by Instagram message, WhatsApp and phone, at all hours, from someone who is comparing you against two or three other clinics in the same fifteen minutes. Response speed is not a courtesy. It is the largest single lever on conversion that a practice of your size controls, and it costs nothing to move.

**Your revenue is a repeat revenue.** Injectables recur at twelve to sixteen weeks. Skin programmes recur at four to six. Laser is sold as a course. A client who is not called back is not a lost visit, she is a lost year. Recall is not marketing, it is the core operating rhythm of the business, and it belongs to the front desk.

**Your risk is reputational and it is personal.** Aesthetics deals with a client's face, at her own expense, with an outcome she judges subjectively in a mirror. When she is disappointed she is disappointed in a way a general practice patient rarely is, and she has an audience. How the desk handles that first hour decides whether it becomes a conversation or a post.

Everything in the two days serves one of those three.

## 2. How the programme is built

Six principles shape it.

- **Objectives first, content second.** Every session is defined by what participants will be *able to do*, not by what will be covered. That is what makes it assessable, and it is what lets you hold the team to it afterwards.
- **Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.** The whole of client experience is divided into five domains. No topic sits in two of them, and together they cover everything that happens around a client. Nothing is taught twice, and nothing that matters is left out.
- **Built on Belfiore, not on a case study.** The role plays are your treatments, your price points, your channels and the situations your team actually meets. The baseline surveys exist so that this is true rather than claimed.
- **Every session names the money.** Each one states which of the three commercial facts above it protects, so the team understands service as the revenue function it is rather than as politeness.
- **Practised, not presented.** Roughly half of the contact time is exercise, role play and live rework of your own messages and scripts.
- **Measured, not assumed.** There is a baseline before the training and an audit after it. Attendance is not evidence that anything changed.

## 3. The frame: five domains

Client experience decomposes cleanly into five domains. The test that separates them is simple and it holds: **before she arrives, while she is with you, when something has gone wrong, after she leaves, and how you know any of it is happening.**

| # | Domain | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **01** | **What good looks like here** | What are we holding ourselves to? |
| **02** | **Doing the job day to day** | How do we handle a client whose visit is going well? |
| **03** | **When it is difficult** | How do we handle one whose visit is not? |
| **04** | **The client who is not in the room** | What happens between visits? |
| **05** | **How we know it is working** | Who checks, how often, and against what? |

> Ten sessions, five hours a day, two days. Every one of the ten sits in exactly one domain. If a question about a client cannot be placed in one of these five, it is not a client experience question.

## 4. Day one

**Domains 01, 02 and the first half of 03. Understanding what good looks like, and doing it.**

### Session 1. Where the money is: the Belfiore client journey

*Domain 01. Protects: conversion.*

We map, on the wall, every point at which a person meets Belfiore, from seeing a post to being called back a year later. Then we mark the four or five moments that actually decide whether she proceeds, returns and refers, because they are not the ones most practices spend their effort on.

**By the end, participants can** describe the Belfiore client journey end to end; name the moments where a client is most likely to be lost; and say what their own role does at each one.

**Practical application:** journey mapping with your own enquiry threads from the last month.

### Session 2. The Belfiore service standard

*Domain 01. Protects: all three.*

You cannot train against nothing, coach against nothing or audit against nothing. The team drafts the standard it will be held to: reply times by channel, who owns an enquiry, what may be decided at the desk and what must be escalated, what is promised and what is never promised.

**By the end, participants can** state the response-time standard for every channel; say what they are authorised to decide alone; and identify what must be escalated and to whom.

**Practical application:** a drafted one-page Belfiore service standard, to come to you for sign-off.

### Session 3. First contact on every channel

*Domain 02. Protects: conversion.*

The enquiry as it actually arrives: an Instagram message at 11pm, a WhatsApp voice note, a phone call during a treatment, a walk-in while two people are already waiting. How to open, how to qualify without interrogating, how to move somebody from a channel into a booked consultation, and how to answer **"how much is it"** without either deflecting or collapsing the price.

**By the end, participants can** run a first contact on any of your four channels to a booked consultation or a recorded outcome; answer a price question with confidence; and handle three or four enquiries at once without losing one.

**Practical application:** live rework of your own recent enquiry threads, then role play on each channel.

### Session 4. In the clinic, from the door to the till

*Domain 02. Protects: retention and reputation.*

Arrival, the wait, the reception area as a private space, the handover to the clinician, the checkout, the payment conversation and the next appointment. The small mechanics that decide whether a first visit feels like a clinic or like a shop.

**By the end, participants can** run an arrival and a checkout to the standard; tell a waiting client what is happening before she has to ask; and close a visit with the next appointment booked.

**Practical application:** a walk-through of your own reception, timed, with the gaps written down.

### Session 5. Confidentiality, consent and discretion

*Domain 02. Protects: reputation and your legal position.*

The part a general customer service course does not contain and an aesthetics practice cannot do without. What the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 requires of you in plain language. Photographs: why consent for the clinical record and consent for marketing are two different permissions, both written, both withdrawable. Personal phones and WhatsApp groups. What may be said at a desk where another client can hear. And the one your team will meet sooner than you think: a caller asking whether a named person is a client here, and why the answer is the same whoever is asking.

**By the end, participants can** state what may and may not be disclosed and to whom; take and record photographic consent correctly; handle a request to confirm a client's attendance; and keep a front-desk conversation private in a small reception.

**Practical application:** eight real scenarios, decided in pairs, then argued out.

## 5. Day two

**The rest of domain 03, then 04 and 05. When it is hard, what happens afterwards, and how it is held in place.**

### Session 6. The difficult client

*Domain 03. Protects: reputation and retention.*

A difficult client is not one thing, and treating her as one thing is why most service training fails at exactly this point. There are six, they are driven by six different things, and the response that works for one makes another worse. Section 6 of this document sets them out in full.

**By the end, participants can** identify which of the six is in front of them within the first minute; select the matching response; hold a boundary without losing warmth; and recover their own composure afterwards.

**Practical application:** six role plays, one per type, drawn from situations your team reports in the baseline survey.

### Session 7. The dissatisfied result

*Domain 03. Protects: reputation and your clinical governance.*

The situation specific to your business: a client who is unhappy with how she looks. It is emotional, it is often clinical, and the front desk is the first to meet it. The rule is precise. **Admin never assesses a clinical outcome and never concedes one.** What the desk does is acknowledge, escalate within a stated time, record accurately, and keep the client with you while the clinician deals with it.

**By the end, participants can** take a result complaint without diagnosing, defending or conceding; escalate it to the right clinician within the standard; record it so it cannot be lost; and manage the hours in between so the client does not go public while she waits.

**Practical application:** the complaint-response framework, then three escalations run live.

### Session 8. Follow-up and aftercare contact

*Domain 04. Protects: retention and reputation.*

What happens in the days after a treatment, and who owns it. The twenty-four hour check, the seven-day check, the aftercare instructions that were given verbally and forgotten before she reached her car, the review request timed to when she can actually see the result rather than when it suits you, and the referral ask made at the only moment it ever works.

**By the end, participants can** run the aftercare contact sequence; write a check-in message that gets a reply; ask for a review and a referral at the right moment; and recognise in a reply the early sign of a complaint.

**Practical application:** the team writes the actual message set, which becomes yours to keep.

### Session 9. Prompting, recall and rebooking

*Domain 04. Protects: revenue, directly.*

The commercial engine of the practice and the part most clinics never systematise. Confirmations, the reminder at seventy-two hours and again at twenty-four, deposits and what they are really for, the recall interval for each of your treatment lines, the rebook offered at checkout rather than chased three months later, and the reactivation of clients nobody has contacted in a year.

**By the end, participants can** operate the recall list; state the return interval for each treatment line; offer the next appointment at checkout; run a reactivation contact; and explain a deposit without apologising for it.

**Practical application:** building your recall calendar, treatment by treatment, and drafting the reactivation message.

### Session 10. Feedback, audit and making it stick

*Domain 05. Protects: all three, permanently.*

How you find out whether any of this is happening when nobody is watching. The feedback instrument and when to send it. Mystery shopping your own front desk. Reviewing calls and messages as a coaching act rather than a disciplinary one. The five numbers worth looking at every week. Then the mechanics of permanence: the fifteen-minute weekly huddle, who owns client experience by name, and each participant's signed thirty-day plan.

**By the end, participants can** run the weekly service huddle; read the five numbers and say what each one means; receive a review of their own call without defensiveness; and state their own thirty-day commitments.

**Practical application:** each participant writes and signs a thirty-day plan. You receive a copy.

## 6. The six difficult clients

This is the section your team will use most, so it is set out in full. The six are distinguished by **what is driving the behaviour**, not by how loud it is. That is what makes the set usable under pressure: one question, asked silently, tells the person at the desk which response to reach for.

| The client | What is driving her | What she needs first | The mistake that makes it worse |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The anxious one** | Fear, not anger. She is about to let someone change her face. | Time, information and an unhurried voice | Efficiency. Being handled quickly reads as being dismissed |
| **The angry one** | A specific failure: a wait, a cancellation, a charge, a botched booking | Acknowledgement of the failure before any explanation | Explaining first. An explanation before an acknowledgement is heard as an excuse |
| **The disappointed one** | A gap between what she expected and what she sees in the mirror | To be taken seriously and moved to the clinician fast | Reassurance from the desk. It is not yours to give and it destroys the clinician's position |
| **The entitled one** | Status. She expects to be an exception | To be recognised, then given a warm and immovable boundary | Bending the rule. The exception becomes the new baseline and the team loses the standard |
| **The negotiator** | Money. She wants a discount and will test for one | Value restated in terms of what she is actually buying | Conceding at the desk, or apologising for the price |
| **The public one** | An audience. She feels she has to be loud to be heard | Speed, a named person, and a move off the public channel | Arguing in public, deleting the comment, or going silent |

**Three rules that run across all six.**

1. **Acknowledge before you explain.** In every one of the six, the first move is the same and almost everyone gets it wrong under pressure.
2. **Name yourself and own it.** "I am Chidinma, I am looking after this, I will come back to you by four o'clock" resolves more than any script. A client who has a name escalates far less often than one who has a department.
3. **Never concede a clinical judgement and never concede a price at the desk.** Both belong to somebody else, and conceding either creates a problem nobody at the desk can undo.

## 7. The service standard you will end up with

This is the shape of what the team drafts in session 2 and you sign. It is not imposed. A standard the team wrote is a standard the team keeps.

| Area | The standard |
|---|---|
| **First response, opening hours** | Instagram and WhatsApp within 30 minutes, never beyond 3 hours. Missed calls returned within 60 minutes. Email the same working day |
| **First response, out of hours** | An acknowledgement on receipt, a real reply by 10am |
| **Ownership** | Every enquiry has one named person until it is closed. Nothing sits in a shared inbox unowned |
| **Outcome** | No enquiry closes without a recorded outcome: booked, declined, or a reason |
| **Price** | The price is given when asked. It is never deflected to "let me check and revert" unless it genuinely varies |
| **Waiting** | No client waits more than 15 minutes without being told why and given a new time |
| **Privacy** | Nothing about a client is said where another client can hear. Attendance is never confirmed to a caller |
| **Complaints** | Logged the day they happen. A result complaint reaches a clinician within the stated time, always |
| **Aftercare** | Every treated client is contacted within 24 hours by a named person |
| **Next appointment** | No client leaves without knowing when to come back, and ideally with the appointment made |

## 8. Measurement: the five numbers

You cannot hold a standard you are not measuring. Five numbers, reviewed weekly, are enough for a practice your size. They take one person about twenty minutes a week once the sheet exists.

| # | The number | Why it is on the list |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Median first response time, by channel** | The single largest controllable lever on conversion, and the one that slips first when the desk is busy |
| 2 | **Enquiries to booked consultations** | Separates a demand problem from a conversion problem. They have opposite answers and opposite costs |
| 3 | **Consultations to treatments** | Tests the consultation itself, and whether expectations are being set honestly |
| 4 | **Rebook rate at checkout** | The cheapest revenue in the business. A rebook made at the till costs nothing; the same booking chased in March costs a campaign |
| 5 | **No-shows and late cancellations** | Directly measures whether prompting and deposits are working, and it is pure lost capacity |

**Two watch items** alongside them: complaints logged and closed within seventy-two hours, and dormant clients reactivated in the month.

**And the audit.** Numbers tell you what happened, not why. A mystery shop, meaning contacts made to your own desk across all four channels by someone your team does not know, tells you what a stranger actually experiences. It is uncomfortable the first time and it is the most useful single input to coaching that exists.

## 9. The follow-up and recall calendar

Session 9 builds this with your team, against your own treatment lines. The shape is this.

| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| On booking | Confirmation with preparation notes and the deposit position | Front desk |
| 72 hours before | First reminder | Automated, checked |
| 24 hours before | Second reminder, with a real reply channel | Automated, checked |
| At checkout | Aftercare given in writing, next appointment offered | Front desk |
| 24 hours after | Aftercare check-in from a named person | Front desk |
| 7 days after | How are you feeling, anything you want us to look at | Front desk |
| At the result point | Review request and, where appropriate, the referral ask | Front desk |
| At the recall interval | Recall contact, by treatment line | Recall list |
| 6 months quiet | Reactivation message | Recall list |
| 12 months quiet | A call from a named person, not a message | Practice manager |

> The recall intervals themselves are clinical and they are yours to set. What we install is the discipline that somebody is on a list, the list is worked, and nobody falls off it because the person who remembered her has gone on leave.
