# PART A: STRATEGY PROPOSAL

## 1. Executive summary

Aman HMO has set a clear target: grow the Lagos provider network from 120 to roughly 300 in Q3. The current tactics (a nationwide email campaign to 3,000 providers, callbacks, and contract business development managers) are all converting poorly.

Our central recommendation is to change the question. "How do we sign 300 providers?" is a volume question, and volume is exactly what email and untargeted field visits are optimised for. The better question is: **"Where do Aman's enrollees actually live, work, and seek care, and which providers do we need there so that every member has good access?"** That is a coverage question, and it produces a very different, much shorter, and far more valuable target list.

Four things make this the right strategy for Aman specifically, and they are the spine of everything that follows:

1. **The seed demand is already knowable.** Aman's initial Lagos enrollees will largely be existing (northern) corporate accounts that have Lagos branches. Aman does not need to guess where demand will land. It can pull the existing book, filter to accounts with Lagos offices, geocode those branch addresses, and that becomes the day-one enrollee map that provider acquisition should anchor to.

2. **The affinity network is a conversion channel email cannot touch.** As a Takaful, Islamic-principles HMO with strong links to Islamic-led organisations, Aman has warm access to institutions email marketing has no route into: large Lagos-rooted Muslim organisations, Islamic-finance employers, faith-aligned hospitals and clinics, and Muslim professional networks. In this segment, trust is carried by warm, faith-fluent introductions, not cold outreach. That is precisely the gap in the current approach.

3. **The real conversion lever is payment reliability, not persuasion.** Lagos providers do not decline HMOs because they have not heard of them. They decline because of low tariffs, delayed payment, denied and reduced claims, and paperwork. A provider proposition led with guaranteed nearby patient volume and a credible prompt-payment commitment attacks the actual reason for "no."

4. **C4A can seed the network with clinicians, not just chase facilities.** C4A operates CadreHealth, a workforce platform of more than 10,000 clinicians, from which around 500 Lagos specialists can be curated for direct partnership with Aman. Because senior specialists practise across several hospitals, each relationship extends member access to more than one facility at once, and the hospital still earns on the facility side. Unbundling pharmacy and diagnostics to dedicated channels then makes the network richer, not just longer. This is the one asset a competitor cannot copy.

The category is real and validated: a Sharia-compliant HMO (Noor Health) already launched in Lagos in November 2024. Aman is not creating a market, it is entering a proven one with a first mover to out-execute. The prize is not 300 signatures. It is a network where a high share of Aman's Lagos members can reach an appropriate, in-network provider quickly, which is what retains corporate accounts and wins the next ones.

**What we ask Aman to change:**

- Measure success as **covered-enrollee percentage and network adequacy in priority clusters**, with the provider count as a secondary output, so the team does not "hit 300" by signing cheap, empty providers where no members are.
- Treat the internal enrollee, account, and provider data as the first deliverable. It is the load-bearing input and it is on the critical path.
- Lead go-to-market with the affinity network and existing-client branches, and back the field team with a payment-reliability proposition the operations side can actually honour.

Because Q3 is already underway (this plan is dated 9 July), the execution window is a compressed sprint of roughly eleven weeks. The plan in Part B is built for that reality.

## 2. The situation, and the reframe

### 2.1 What the brief says

- **Problem:** Aman is losing business in Lagos due to a thin provider network, despite a competitive benefits package and good service.
- **Objective:** onboard about 300 providers in Lagos in Q3 (from a base of 120).
- **Current tactics:** a nationwide email campaign to 3,000 providers (low conversion), callbacks to those who expressed interest, and contract field managers (low conversion).

The diagnosis in the brief is correct: a thin network is costing accounts. The tactics, however, are built for reach, and reach is not the constraint.

### 2.2 Why the current approach underperforms

Three reasons, each supported by the market evidence:

**It is national when the problem is local.** Emailing 3,000 providers nationwide to solve a Lagos coverage problem spreads effort across the whole country and across providers who may sit nowhere near an Aman enrollee. A provider onboarded in an LGA with no Aman members is a credentialing cost with no retention benefit.

**It leads with awareness when the barrier is economics.** In Lagos, providers participate in insurance because it is profitable: the stated motivators are more patients, revenue growth, and a constant monthly inflow that funds the business. They decline for the opposite reasons: low tariffs, delayed and denied claims, paperwork, and poor HMO responsiveness. An email does not move any of those levers. Grievance over HMO payment practices is severe and well documented in Lagos, to the point of provider associations threatening mass contract termination in 2022 over trapped claims. A generic pitch into that environment lands on scepticism.

**It ignores Aman's two structural advantages.** The existing corporate book and the affinity network are the two assets a generic HMO does not have, and neither is expressed in an email blast.

### 2.3 The reframe: a coverage problem, not a volume problem

The strategy rests on a single shift:

> Provider acquisition is a **derived function** of where Aman's enrollees and corporate accounts are, and of where the network has adequacy gaps. It is not a standalone number to be maximised.

Under this reframe:

- The **target list is generated, not canvassed.** You start from enrollee and branch geography, apply network-adequacy standards, find the gaps, and the providers you need fall out of that analysis as a named, ranked list.
- **Quality of coverage beats quantity of signatures.** Three hundred providers scattered where no members are is worse than 180 providers positioned exactly where members seek care.
- **The affinity network is the way in,** because a warm introduction through a shared institution converts where a cold email does not.

The rest of Part A builds this out: the four pillars, the market evidence underneath them, the operating model that combines them, and how to measure it.

## 3. The four strategic pillars

### Pillar 1: Data-led geographic targeting, anchored to enrollees and branches

Aman should build its Lagos network outward from demand, not inward from a provider list.

**The method:**

1. **Map demand.** Geocode every Lagos enrollee's location and, critically, every Lagos office or branch of Aman's existing corporate accounts. Because the seed book is existing northern clients with Lagos branches, this map can be produced in days from data Aman already holds.
2. **Overlay adequacy standards.** Set localised time-and-distance and provider-to-enrollee targets for each cluster (see 4.3). This turns "we feel thin in Lekki" into "we are two secondary facilities short of adequacy in the Lekki-Ajah corridor for 900 lives."
3. **Find the gaps and rank them.** Weight gaps by how many enrollees and corporate lives they affect. The largest, most under-served clusters become the acquisition priority.
4. **Target the right providers into the gaps.** Not any provider, but the ones that close the specific tier gap in that cluster, filtered for conversion propensity (see 4.2).

This converts a vague target of 300 into a prioritised, named, and defensible acquisition map. It also gives the field team a reason a provider can hear: "we are approaching you because a specific, countable number of our members are next to you right now."

### Pillar 2: Affinity-led distribution through the Takaful and Islamic network

This is Aman's genuine differentiator, and it operates on both sides of the network.

**On the enrollee side**, Aman can grow accounts through channels a generic HMO cannot enter:
- Large Lagos-rooted Muslim organisations with dense, physically-anchored footprints (for example NASFAT, which reports over a million members, was founded in Lagos, holds weekly gatherings at the Alausa secretariat in Ikeja, and has branches across Lagos LGAs; and FOMWAN, a Muslim women's association with tens of thousands of members and its own clinics).
- Islamic-finance employers with real Lagos presence as corporate accounts (Lotus Bank, headquartered in Ikoyi; The Alternative Bank on Ikorodu Road, Ilupeju; the Lagos-based Takaful insurers). These are natural faith-aligned accounts and warm-introduction hubs.

**On the provider side**, the same network yields anchor providers and trusted introductions:
- Faith-based organisations that already run hospitals and clinics (for example FOMWAN's facilities, concentrated in maternal and reproductive care) can serve as anchor providers and enrollee touchpoints in Muslim-concentrated LGAs.
- Muslim medical professional networks provide warm routes to provider owners and principals, so the first contact is a peer introduction rather than a cold call.

**Why this works:** in this segment, agents and trusted intermediaries are the first and primary source of information about the product, and a documented awareness and trust barrier (a long-standing belief among many Muslims that conventional insurance is not permissible) makes faith-fluent, association-based introductions materially more effective than cold outreach. The Takaful model itself (cooperative, mutual, surplus-sharing, avoiding interest and excessive uncertainty) is a trust asset when it is explained by someone the provider or employer already trusts.

**An important geographic bonus:** the affinity map and the corporate-demand map overlap. NASFAT's headquarters sits in Ilupeju, its weekly hub is in Ikeja/Alausa, Lotus Bank anchors Ikoyi, The Alternative Bank anchors Ilupeju, and Muslim-dense communities extend through Ikorodu, Agege, Alimosho, Mushin, and Lagos Island. Several of these coincide with mainland and island corporate corridors, so affinity-led and demand-led targeting reinforce each other rather than competing.

### Pillar 3: A provider value proposition that actually converts

The field team should not be selling "join our network." It should be offering the three things the evidence says providers actually want, in this order:

1. **Guaranteed nearby volume.** "We have N members within Y kilometres of your facility today." This is only possible because of Pillar 1, and it is the single most persuasive opening.
2. **Payment reliability.** A clear, published commitment on claims turnaround, with the operational backing to honour it. This directly attacks the number-one stated reason providers decline and exit HMOs. It must not be promised unless operations can deliver it, because a broken payment promise destroys trust faster than no promise at all.
3. **Low-friction claims and ethical alignment.** Simple submission and, for faith-aligned providers, the Takaful surplus-sharing and mutual-benefit story, which is a real point of difference from conventional HMOs.

Tiering and incentives (Part B, section 5) sit on top of this. But the proposition, not the persuasion, is what converts.

### Pillar 4: Seed the network with clinicians, through CadreHealth, and unbundle the value chain

This is the pillar only C4A can supply, and it changes both the speed and the shape of the network. Rather than building the network only as a list of contracted facilities, Aman can build it around clinicians directly.

**The lever.** C4A operates CadreHealth, a workforce platform of more than 10,000 clinicians. From it we can curate an initial cohort of around 500 Lagos-based specialists for direct partnership with Aman, a warm, pre-vetted starting network rather than a cold canvass.

**Why clinician-first accelerates coverage:**
- **Warm and fast.** These clinicians are already in the C4A network, so onboarding is a curated introduction, not a cold approach.
- **A coverage multiplier.** Senior Lagos specialists typically hold appointments and visiting rights across several private hospitals. Contracting the specialist therefore extends member access to more than one facility at once, so each relationship closes multiple geographic and tier gaps rather than one.
- **The hospital still wins, so it is an easier partner.** When a partnered specialist brings an Aman member into a hospital, the facility still earns on the bed, theatre, nursing, diagnostics, and pharmacy. This is added insured volume, not displaced business, which reframes the facility conversation from "join our HMO" to "here is patient flow you would not otherwise have."

**Then unbundle the rest, so the network is richer, not just longer.** A member does not need "a hospital," they need a specialist to see them, a place to be treated, medicines, and diagnostics. Source each where it is strongest:
- **Pharmacy and consumables.** Capture the last mile through Aman's own pharmacy vehicle or a distribution partnership, for margin and dependable supply, rather than leaving it inside every facility contract.
- **Diagnostics.** Arrange through dedicated partners (for example Beacon HealthCare Diagnostics) selected for quality, price, and reach, rather than accepting whatever each facility offers.

**How it composes with the other pillars.** The specialist cohort seeds coverage fast (Pillar 4); the demand map decides which specialists and clusters to prioritise (Pillar 1); the affinity network supplies warm introductions, including to Muslim medical professional networks (Pillar 2); and the payment-reliability proposition is what makes both specialists and facilities say yes (Pillar 3). The four reinforce each other.

**A commercial note for C4A (internal).** This model routes real activity through C4A's own platforms: specialist placements through CadreHealth, and potential diagnostics and pharmaceutical distribution partnerships. The Aman engagement can therefore pull those platforms along with it. Keep C4A IP and the CadreHealth network licensed in via the engagement, not contributed away.

## 4. The Lagos market: the evidence underneath the strategy

### 4.1 The provider supply is private, urban, and short on the higher tiers

Lagos health-facility supply is overwhelmingly private-for-profit (about 83 percent), heavily urban (about 77 percent), and dominated by primary care: roughly 75 percent of facilities are primary, about 25 percent secondary, and a fraction of a percent tertiary.

**Implication for Aman:** the network will be private-sector-dependent, which is fine. But secondary (inpatient and specialist) capacity is comparatively scarce, so it must be acquired deliberately and early. If the team chases the easy, abundant primary-care signatures, the network will look large on paper while members still cannot find inpatient or specialist care in-network, which is exactly what loses corporate accounts.

### 4.2 Not all providers are equally winnable: a conversion-propensity segmentation

A Lagos study found that about 40 percent of surveyed facilities accept no insurance at all, and that participation is far higher among specific segments:
- **By tier:** secondary and tertiary facilities participate at roughly 77 to 100 percent, versus about 32 percent for primary.
- **By age:** facilities more than 10 years old participate at roughly 71 to 75 percent, versus around 31 to 33 percent for newer ones.
- **By ownership:** private about 67 percent versus public about 47 percent.

**Implication for Aman:** the target list should be weighted toward established, larger, secondary and tertiary private facilities in priority clusters. These are both the scarce tier the network needs and the segment most likely to say yes. This is how the plan hits adequacy faster with fewer, better targets. (The underlying survey is a few years old and a small sample, so treat the pattern as structural direction, not exact current percentages.)

### 4.3 Network adequacy gives a ready-made targeting framework

Managed-care systems measure network adequacy with three quantitative tools: time-and-distance standards, provider-to-enrollee ratios, and appointment wait-time standards. As a working benchmark to localise, dense-metro standards elsewhere target roughly 90 percent of members within about 10 minutes and 5 miles of a primary-care provider, and on the order of 1.67 primary-care physicians per 1,000 members.

These are international benchmarks, not Nigerian regulation, and Lagos traffic and facility mix mean the travel-time thresholds in particular need local calibration. But the framework itself is directly transferable: it is how Aman converts an enrollee map into a defensible number of providers needed per cluster, by tier. Part B turns this into a concrete scoring model.

### 4.4 The competitive field, including a validated Takaful precedent

The Lagos HMO market is competitive and incumbents already densify across the main corridors (Lekki, Victoria Island, Ikeja, Surulere, Gbagada, Ikorodu, Ajah, Festac, Isolo, Agege, Yaba, Apapa). Corporate demand is tightest in prime office submarkets such as Ikeja GRA and Victoria Island.

The most strategically important competitive fact is category-level: **Noor Takaful launched a Sharia-compliant HMO, Noor Health, in Lagos in November 2024**, and the Takaful sector already has a Lagos footprint (two of the five licensed Takaful operators are Lagos-headquartered). This cuts two ways. It validates Aman's exact product and de-risks the category. And it means Aman has a first mover in the affinity niche and must move with intent, competing on the quality of its network, its payment reliability, and the depth of its community relationships rather than on novelty.

### 4.5 The regulatory context is a tailwind for a reliability-led player

Two regulatory facts matter:

- **The NHIA Act 2022** reshaped the HMO model: fund management moved from HMOs toward state schemes, and the framework for third-party administrators was widened to include mutual health associations, a category that sits naturally alongside a cooperative Takaful framing. Aman should verify current NHIA operational guidance during design, and explore whether a mutual-aligned structure offers any trust or regulatory advantage.
- **Payment discipline is now being enforced.** In 2024 and 2025, the NHIA sanctioned dozens of HMOs and delisted or suspended facilities over poor service and payment delays, and approved the first actuarial tariff reset in over a decade. These are public-scheme tariffs rather than commercial HMO rates, so they reset the market floor and the mood rather than Aman's own pricing. The direction of travel rewards exactly the payment-reliability position in Pillar 3.

The four pillars combine into a single repeatable funnel that the team runs cluster by cluster:

  [1] DEMAND MAP  <--------------+                        |

        v                                                 |
  [2] ADEQUACY OVERLAY                                     |
        |   time-distance + provider-to-enrollee, by tier |
        v                                                 |
  [3] GAP + PRIORITY RANKING                               |

The loop matters: as providers activate, coverage improves, the adequacy overlay is recomputed, and the next-priority gap is targeted. The team is always working the highest-impact gap, not the next name on an alphabetical list.

## 6. Redefining success: the metrics that matter

We recommend Aman track a small scorecard, with the provider count demoted to an output rather than the goal:

**Primary (coverage and access):**
- **Covered-enrollee percentage:** share of Aman Lagos members within the localised time-and-distance standard of an in-network provider of the appropriate tier. This is the true measure of whether the network is doing its job.
- **Cluster adequacy:** for each priority cluster, provider-to-enrollee ratio and secondary/inpatient access against target.

**Secondary (throughput and quality):**
- **Activation rate:** providers contracted and actually seeing members, not merely signed.
- **Payment SLA adherence:** percentage of claims settled within the committed window. This is the leading indicator of retention and of whether the field promise is credible.
- **Warm-introduction conversion:** conversion rate of affinity-sourced targets versus cold-sourced, to prove and tune the channel.

**Output:**
- **Net provider adds toward 300**, reported alongside coverage so the two are never confused.

The single sentence to give Aman's board: *we would rather reach 250 providers that cover 90 percent of our members than 300 that cover 60 percent.*

## 1. The data request to Aman (critical path, week 1)

Nothing in the demand map can start until this data arrives, so this is the first thing to secure, ideally in the kickoff. Confirm who owns the book: the Lagos office or northern head office, and who can extract it.

**Request the following from Aman:**

| # | Dataset | Fields needed | Why it matters |
|---|---------|---------------|----------------|
| 1 | Enrollee register (Lagos) | Member ID, plan, residential area/LGA (address or nearest landmark), corporate account link | Builds the demand map; residential access matters as much as office access |
| 2 | Corporate accounts | Company, plan, active lives, and **every Lagos office/branch address** | The seed demand anchor; branch geography drives provider priority |
| 3 | Current 120 providers | Name, type, tier, address/LGA, specialties, contract and tariff terms, status (active/inactive) | Baseline coverage; shows existing gaps and dead weight |

| 5 | Current pipeline | Providers emailed/called/visited, stage, objections logged | Salvage warm leads; learn the real objections |
| 6 | Access complaints and churn | Enrollee complaints of "no provider near me," account losses and stated reasons | Confirms the highest-pain clusters; ties coverage to revenue at risk |

**If a dataset is unavailable,** proxy it: geocode corporate branches from public addresses, use HEFAMAA registration data for provider supply by LGA, and flag the assumption. But push hard for items 2, 3, and 7, which are load-bearing and which only Aman holds.

## 2. The provider target scoring model

Each candidate provider gets a weighted score. Tune weights after seeing the data; suggested starting weights below.

| Factor | What it measures | Weight |
|--------|------------------|--------|
| Enrollee demand proximity | Aman members + corporate lives within X km of the provider | 30% |
| Adequacy gap severity | Size of the tier-specific coverage gap in that cluster | 20% |
| Tier fit | Prioritise scarce secondary/inpatient/specialist over abundant primary | 15% |
| Conversion propensity | Established (10+ yrs), secondary/tertiary, private, already accepts insurance | 15% |
| Affinity signal | Faith-aligned/Muslim-owned/association-linked, or a warm introduction is available | 15% |
| Quality and credentialing | HEFAMAA registered, no red flags, credentialable | 5% |

The output is a ranked, named list per cluster. The field team works it top-down, never alphabetically.

## 4. The affinity channel plan

Run these in parallel with the field effort; they generate the warm introductions the field team converts.

**Institutional partnerships (enrollee and warm-intro side):**
- **NASFAT:** pursue a member health-cover conversation and a presence at the Alausa gatherings; ask for introductions to NASFAT-affiliated clinics as providers.
- **FOMWAN:** engage as both an enrollee pool (women and families) and an anchor-provider network in maternal and reproductive care; map their Lagos facilities as candidate providers.
- **Islamic-finance employers:** approach Lotus Bank, The Alternative Bank, Jaiz, TAJBank, and the Lagos Takaful insurers as faith-aligned corporate accounts and co-marketing partners.
- **Muslim medical professional networks:** use these for peer introductions to provider owners, so first contact is warm.

**Community anchors (enrollee side, Muslim-dense LGAs):** identify major mosques and Islamic centres in the west-mainland and Ikorodu zones as enrollment touchpoints.

**Discipline:** every affinity relationship must produce either named enrollee leads or named provider introductions, tracked in the CRM. Relationship-building without a named output is not progress. Verify each institution's actual health facilities and current status before pitching them as anchor providers; several are smaller clinics rather than full hospitals, and at least one publicised faith-based hospital is still aspirational.

## 5. The BDM field playbook

**Sequencing (warm-first):**
1. Affinity or peer introduction lands the meeting.
2. BDM visits with a facility-specific opening: "N Aman members are within Y km of you today."
3. Proposition: guaranteed volume, payment-reliability SLA, low-friction claims, and for faith-aligned providers the Takaful ethical-alignment story.
4. Leave-behind and same-week follow-up (WhatsApp is appropriate here).
5. Credentialing pack collected, contract, activation.

**The conversion funnel to track per BDM per week:**

```
Targeted -> Contacted -> Visited -> Docs submitted -> Contracted -> Activated (seeing members)
```

Report drop-off at each stage. The two stages that usually leak are Visited to Docs submitted (fix with on-the-spot credentialing help) and Contracted to Activated (fix with fast onboarding and the first payment landing on time).

**Cadence:** weekly zone stand-ups, a shared live target list, and weekly recomputation of coverage so the team sees the map improve. Log every objection verbatim; objections are the product feedback that tunes the proposition and the SLA.

**Objection handling anchors:**
- "HMOs don't pay on time" -> the published SLA and the honest current turnaround; do not oversell beyond what operations confirmed.
- "Tariffs are too low" -> lead with volume and reliability; escalate genuine tariff gaps to Aman rather than bluffing.
- "I've never heard of Aman" -> the affinity and Takaful story, delivered by the trusted introducer.

## 6. The clinician-led build: specialists, facilities, pharmacy, diagnostics

The clinician-first model (Part A, Pillar 4) runs as its own track alongside the facility field playbook. C4A leads it because C4A owns the specialist supply.

**Specialist cohort (via CadreHealth).**
- Curate around 500 Lagos-based specialists from CadreHealth, filtered by specialty against the tier gaps in the demand map (prioritise the scarce secondary, inpatient, and specialist needs, Part A section 4.1) and by the clusters where members concentrate.
- Contract the specialist directly for consultation and procedure access, on the visiting-consultant model that already governs how Nigerian consultants practise across several private hospitals. One specialist agreement can therefore activate access at each hospital where they hold rights.
- Credential once (via CadreHealth records) and map each specialist to the facilities they cover, so the network view is specialist-and-facility, not facility-only.

**Facility ancillary terms (the hospital still earns).**
- For each hospital a partnered specialist uses, agree the ancillary terms for bed, theatre, nursing, and observation, so the facility is paid for what it provides when an Aman member is treated there.
- This is the opening line with the hospital: not "join our HMO," but "here is insured patient flow through Dr X, and here is how you are paid for the facility side." It converts facilities a cold pitch would not.
- Verify multi-hospital rights per specialist during mobilisation; do not assume coverage that is not contracted.

**Pharmacy and consumables (capture the last mile).**
- Route dispensing through Aman's own pharmacy vehicle where it exists, or a distribution partner, rather than leaving pharmacy inside every facility contract. This captures margin, standardises the formulary, and protects supply reliability, which also underwrites the member experience.
- Coordinate with the Family Clinics rollout so pharmacy is not duplicated where Aman's own clinics will dispense.

**Diagnostics (arranged through partners).**
- Contract dedicated diagnostics partners (for example Beacon HealthCare Diagnostics) for quality, price, and reach, and steer members to them, rather than defaulting to each facility's in-house lab.
- Prioritise partners whose branch network overlaps the priority clusters, so diagnostics access tracks the same coverage map.

**Track it.** Add to the scorecard: specialists activated, hospitals reached through them (the coverage multiplier), and pharmacy and diagnostics coverage per cluster. Report these alongside facility contracts so the richer network is visible, not hidden behind a raw provider count.

## 7. The provider value proposition and onboarding SLA

Work with Aman operations to fix and publish:
- **Claims turnaround commitment** (a specific number of days), backed by the operational capacity to meet it. This is the centrepiece. Confirm the honest current baseline (data request item 7) before committing to a number.
- **Simple claims submission** and a single responsive relationship contact per zone.
- **Provider tiering** with incentives for priority-tier and priority-cluster providers.
- **The Takaful surplus-sharing narrative** as an ethical differentiator for faith-aligned providers.

Rule: never let the field promise exceed what operations can deliver. A missed payment promise costs more trust than it ever bought.
